Comparison and change over time

Bars for categories, a line for time

A line says "this follows from that", which is only true when the x-axis has an order. Genres have none, so they get bars. Months do, so they get a line, and the two summer valleys jump out of 24 numbers.

The quietest months are June to August 2025: 59, 59 and 57 plays. The busiest is October 2024 with 127. The months here are text; part two redraws this with real dates and the axis gets much tidier.

Bars start at zero

Skip rate per device, every number correct in both. The length of a bar is the number, so cutting the bottom off makes 14.0% look many times bigger than 10.6%. Out of 336 speaker plays, the real difference is about eleven skips.

This is one of the two blocks never to cut. matplotlib starts bar axes at zero by default; the left chart needed set_ylim(10, 14.5) to do the damage. So the lie takes effort, which is worth saying. For lines you can sometimes argue for zooming in. For bars, never.

plt.subplots(1, 2) gives two charts, left and right: the first time the ax. style earns its keep.

Distribution

A histogram for a spread, and "out of what?"

Bins of one minute; the bars touch because minutes have no gaps. The first bar is too tall for the shape: 225 plays under a minute. Stacking the skips on top checks the hunch instead of assuming it.

◆

Out of what? 190 plays are under a minute and skipped.

  • 73.6% of the 258 skips are short
  • 84.4% of the 225 short plays are skips
  • line at two minutes: 98.4% against 65.0%

The other never-cut block. Read the two sentences aloud, "most skips are short" and "most short plays are skips", and ask which the chart supports. "Short" is under one minute, minutes_played < 1. Then move the line to two minutes: the first becomes 98.4% and the second drops to 65.0%. A percentage in a title needs its "out of what".

What it does not show: why a play was short. 35 plays under a minute were not skips.

Every chart answers one question, and a reader happily takes it as the answer to a nearby one.

On a pie you compare angles of slices that start in different places. Nobody does that well: Latin and Afrobeat are 185 and 180 plays and their slices look the same. Bars compare lengths from one baseline, which everybody does well. A pie is fine for two or three slices; past that, bars.

Change over time

Real dates, and telling the reader where to look

Real dates: matplotlib spaces the ticks by time and nothing needs turning sideways. A missing month would show as a gap, not be squeezed out.

The title, "Every summer falls below the average of 91.0 plays a month", is checked in the notebook: all six summer months are below it.

resample("MS") is the session 6 tool, MS for month start. f"{peak_date:%B %Y}" is one more f-string format: a date takes date codes after the colon, so the label says "October 2024".

Notebook 02 then puts 2024 and 2025 on top of each other by month of the year, 2024 in grey and 2025 in colour, labelled at the line ends instead of with a legend: 2025 had a quieter summer (175 plays against 201) and a busier autumn (337 against 313).

The one that matters

Grey everything, colour the one the title is about

"Folk lost the most plays: 236 in 2024, 202 in 2025." The same eight lines as the spaghetti, and nothing to decode.

The recipe
  1. everything in grey, thin
  2. the one the title names last, in colour, thick
  3. label it at the line end, not in a legend
  4. annotate the moment that makes the point

The biggest fall is spring 2025: 39 plays against 58 a year before. The annotation in the notebook uses a date and a number for xytext too, to put the text in the one empty part of the chart.

Small multiples show every genre; highlighting answers a question about one. Ask which he would use for "how did each genre do?" and which for "which genre fell most?".

Colour for every reader

Colour should mean something, and never work alone

  • One job per colour. One for the data, grey for context, one strong colour for the point. Not a colour per bar to look nice.
  • Never colour alone. Label lines directly, write numbers on bars.
  • Not red against green. One man in twelve cannot tell them apart. Blue against orange is safe.
  • Differ in lightness, so it survives a greyscale printer.
  • Amounts: light to dark in one colour ("Blues"), never a rainbow.

The greyscale test

Save the stacked histogram, read the PNG back with plt.imread, mix it to grey. Part one's blue and red differ in lightness by 0.07: two near-identical greys. Okabe-Ito blue and orange: 0.29.

The greyscale test is the one practical check he can always run: you cannot see your chart the way everyone else does, but you can see it without colour. Lightness is 0.299 red + 0.587 green + 0.114 blue, because the eye sees green as much brighter.

Honest admission worth making: the course's own part-one histogram fails the test. Only the white edges keep the bars apart.

Ready for someone else

One figure, four panels, one title over all of it

  • each panel title gives its own answer
  • the same blue means "the data" in all four, orange the point
  • layout="constrained": no title overlaps another
  • dpi=200 for print, 150 for slides, 100 for screens

Over 2,000 pixels wide. If anything is cramped, make figsize bigger, not the fonts smaller. .svg instead of .png stays sharp at any size.

Unpacking axes into four names is what makes this readable: every line says which panel it draws on. With four panels, the ax. style is not a preference any more.
