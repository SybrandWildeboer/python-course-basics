Part one · the first hour

Cleaning

The part of the job nobody puts in the job description.

Setting expectations

This is most of the work, and it is not a detour

Every dataset you have been given so far was clean, which is not what data is like. Real files have missing values, three spellings of the same word, dates in two formats, numbers stored as text, and rows that appear twice.

People who work with data spend most of their time here. It is not the boring bit before the interesting bit. It is where the answers get decided, because what you do about a gap changes the number you report.

The one thing to take from today

Every cleaning step is a decision, not a rule. Dropping rows with no duration and filling them with the average give different answers. Neither is wrong. What is wrong is not noticing you chose.

So: write down what you did and why. Your future self will want it, and so will anybody who has to trust your number.

Do not let this become gloomy. The framing that lands is that cleaning is where the judgement lives, which makes it the interesting part rather than the chore.

Do it now

Meet the mess

▲

Two things are already wrong, and one is invisible

2,223 rows, when the clean file has 2,183. And look at the last column name: minutes played . A space instead of an underscore, and a trailing space. df["minutes_played"] raises a KeyError and you will stare at it for a while.

Have them type df["minutes_played"] and hit the KeyError, then df.columns.tolist() to see the real name with the space. It is a five-minute confusion the first time it happens for real.

Diagnose before you fix

value_counts() on every category

◆

Four spellings of Electronic: Electronic, ELECTRONIC, electronic, and " Electronic " with spaces around it. To pandas those are four completely different genres, so every groupby("genre") you have written would silently split them into four rows.

◆

This is the habit from session 6's DISTINCT slide, arriving where it matters. Run value_counts(dropna=False) on every categorical column of any new dataset. It takes ten seconds and it is how you find this.

dropna=False matters: without it the NaN row is hidden, and the missing values are exactly what you are looking for.

Fix 1 and 2

Renaming, and cleaning text

.str is how you reach the text

A whole column at a time. .str.strip(), .str.lower(), .str.title(), .str.replace(), .str.contains(). They are the ordinary string methods, applied to every value.

▲

Strip before you compare, always. " Pop " and "Pop" are different strings, and the difference is invisible on screen. It is the single most annoying bug in this whole area.

.str.title() on "hip-hop" gives "Hip-Hop", which may or may not be what they want. Mention that the choice of which capitalisation to standardise on is itself a decision.

Fix 3

Numbers that arrived as text

errors="coerce" is the important bit

Anything it cannot read becomes NaN instead of stopping your program. That turns a crash into a countable problem, and counting it is how you decide what to do next.

The related tools: .astype(int) when you are certain, pd.to_numeric when you are not, and pd.to_datetime for dates, which is the next slide and is worse than it looks.

Always check .isna().sum() after a conversion. If it jumped, your cleaning just created missing values.

Without errors="coerce" this raises and stops. With it, you get a number you can act on. That difference is worth spelling out: coerce converts a blocking error into information.

Fix 4 · the one that matters

The convenient way to parse dates is silently wrong

■

It corrupts 628 of the 2,183 rows

dayfirst=True is applied to every value, including the ISO ones. 2025-09-06, which is the 6th of September, gets read as the 9th of June. 29% of your dates are now wrong, and nothing told you.

This is the best slide in the session. The wrong version is more convenient, runs without complaint, and produces a plausible date for every row. The only way to catch it is to know the formats in your data and parse them deliberately.

The check that proves it: clean the messy file and compare play by play against plays.csv. The notebook does exactly that.

Fix 5

Duplicates

Look before you delete

keep=False shows you all copies rather than just the extras, so you can see what is actually repeated. Sometimes two identical rows are two real events.

Here they are genuinely accidental: 40 rows copied during an export, identical down to the play_id, which is supposed to be unique. That last fact is what makes it safe to say they are duplicates.

2,223 back to 2,183. The clean file has 2,183 rows, which is a satisfying moment and a useful cross-check.

The primary key argument matters: without a unique id, two identical rows might be two real plays of the same track at the same time. Knowing the data is what tells you.

Fix 6 · the judgement call

Missing values: drop, fill, or label?

Drop the rows

For the 94 plays with no duration. A play with no minutes cannot answer a question about minutes.

Fill with a value

Keeps every row and invents data. Defensible for a count, dishonest for a total.

Label it

For the 70 missing devices. Losing a whole play because nobody recorded the device would be worse.

■

These three give three different answers, and all three are defensible

Drop the 94 and your total is 8,608 minutes. Fill them with the average and it is about 8,995. Neither number is wrong. What would be wrong is reporting one without knowing you chose it. Write the choice down.

The two totals are the whole lesson of the session in one line. Put both on screen and ask which they would report, and to whom.

A rule of thumb worth giving: drop when the missing value makes the row useless for your question; label when the row is still useful; fill only when you can defend the invented value out loud.

A trap worth knowing

pandas decides some things are missing without asking

Where did they go?

read_csv treats a list of strings as missing by default, including NA, N/A, null, NaN, and an empty cell. Helpful most of the time.

And sometimes badly wrong

If NA in your data means Namibia, or North America, pandas has just deleted a real value and called it missing. The same goes for a product code like NULL.

The fix is to know your data. keep_default_na=False takes the file literally, and na_values=[...] lets you say exactly what counts as missing.

This one delights people, because it is a helpful default that can quietly destroy data. It is also a good argument for always reading the raw file once before trusting any loader.

Before you move on

Check what your cleaning threw away

■

6% is fine. 60% would not be.

It is genuinely common to write a cleaning step that silently discards most of the data, especially a dropna() with no subset, which drops a row if any column is empty. Here that would have cost you 238 rows instead of 94.

So print the shape before and after. Every time. It is one line and it is the difference between cleaning your data and quietly deleting it.

Demonstrate a bare dropna() to see the row count fall further. It is the single most common accidental data loss in pandas.

Part two · the second hour

Charts

The data is clean. Now make it say something.

The structure

A figure, and axes inside it

Two objects, and the difference matters

The figure is the picture: its size, and saving it. The axes are the plot inside it: the data, the labels, the limits. Almost everything you do is ax.something().

You will see tutorials using plt.plot() with no fig or ax. It works, and it breaks down the moment you want two charts. Learn the fig, ax version once and never think about it again.

tight_layout() stops your labels being cut off at the edges. Add it out of habit.

fig, ax = plt.subplots() is the one line to memorise. Everything else is discoverable from there.

Choosing

Four charts, and when each one is right

ChartUse it forIn our data
Line ax.plotChange over time Minutes per month across the two years
Bar ax.barComparing categories Total minutes per genre, or per device
Scatter ax.scatterRelationship between two numbersPlays per artist against their total minutes
Histogram ax.histDistribution of one numberHow play lengths are spread, and where the skips sit

Time on the x-axis wants a line. A bar chart of 24 months is harder to read and says the same thing.

Categories want bars, sorted. Alphabetical order is almost never the order the reader wants; biggest first usually is.

The sorted-bars point is small and makes charts noticeably better. Sort by value unless the category has a natural order, like days of the week.

Non-negotiable

A chart without labels is not finished

What an unlabelled chart says

"Here is a shape. You work out what it means, what the units are, and when it is from." Nobody reading it can check whether you are right, which means nobody should believe it.

The minimum, every time
  • a title that says the finding, not the columns
  • both axis labels, with units
  • a legend, if there is more than one line
◆

Titles: say the finding

"Minutes by month" tells the reader what the axes already tell them. "Listening drops off every summer" tells them what you found. If you cannot write the second kind, you may not have found anything yet, and that is useful to notice.

◆

Your homework is three charts plus a findings.md with two or three sentences per chart. The sentences are the point; the charts are the evidence.

The "title says the finding" rule transforms beginner charts. It also exposes charts that show nothing, which is a useful side effect.

Practical

savefig, and why saving beats showing

In a notebook, charts just appear

Which is why notebooks are so good for this work. You still want savefig when the chart needs to go into a document, an email, or a repo.

In a script, plt.show() blocks

It opens a window and waits for you to close it. That is fine once and useless in anything scheduled or automated, where nobody is there to click. Save instead.

dpi=150 is a good default: sharp on a screen, small enough to email. output/ is in .gitignore, because a generated file can always be regenerated.

The Agg backend point saves a real headache the first time they run a chart script on a machine with no display, which is every server they will ever meet.

Ethics, briefly

The easiest way to mislead with a chart

Average minutes per device

car 4.38, phone 4.18, laptop 4.09, speaker 3.99. Starting the axis at zero: four bars of nearly identical height, because the difference between highest and lowest is about 10%. Starting it at 3.9: the car bar towers over the speaker, and it looks like a finding.

Bar charts should start at zero. The length of a bar is the quantity, so cutting the bottom off lies about the ratio. Line charts have more latitude, since they are about change rather than magnitude, but say so if you crop.

This happens by accident at least as often as on purpose, because some plotting tools crop by default to "use the space well". Check your y-axis before you send a chart to anybody.

Make both versions live, side by side, with their own data. It takes two minutes and it is the sort of thing people remember for years.
