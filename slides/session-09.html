Three tools find nearly all of it: info() for types and gaps, value_counts(dropna=False) on every text column, and duplicated().sum(). Run them on any new file before asking it a single question.

The numbers here are for the file as loaded with a plain read_csv, all 2,223 rows. Build this table with them on the whiteboard rather than revealing it: ask "what else?" until they run out, then fill the gaps.

The generator can also write thousands with a space, like 1 234.50, but no play in this log lasts 1,000 minutes, so it never appears. Mention it only if asked.

Fixes 1 and 2

phone, PHONE and phone are three words

.str gives you the text methods from session 1, applied to every row at once. One line, and the phone count goes up by 225.

value_counts() leaves out missing values unless you say dropna=False. Without it, 70 plays silently vanish from the table.

Session 6 promised this moment. Point back to it: "phone and PHONE is what session 9 is about." (In this file it is phone, PHONE and "phone " with a trailing space.)

The trailing space in "minutes played " is invisible to print. print(repr(df.columns[-1])) shows the quotes, and the space before the closing one. It is the tool for any value that "is obviously right" and still does not match.

The bug of the day is forgetting df = or df["device"] = . Nearly every pandas method returns a new object. When "my fix did nothing", that is why.

Fix 3

errors="coerce" never complains, so you have to

convert straight away
clean the text first, then convert

coerce turns anything it cannot read into NaN. The column had 94 empty cells; the straight conversion made 190. The other 96 were good numbers like "4.4 min", thrown away without a word. That is 436.7 minutes.

The rule: count the missing values before and after. If the number went up, find out why before you go on. In the notebook an assert does it: fixed.isna().sum() == 94.

This is the first number on the ledger that moves for a reason the learner chose. Make them say both totals out loud. 8,333.2 looks just as plausible as 8,769.9, and that is the problem.

The ValueError is a good error

pandas guessed the format from the first row, met a row that did not fit, and stopped. Loud and honest.

format="mixed" gets 159 dates wrong

It reads 03/09/2025 month first, the American way. It only gets 16/08/2024 right because there is no 16th month.

430 European dates. 174 have a day of 12 or less, so they fit either way round. 15 of those have the same day and month. 174 − 15 = 159.

Do the arithmetic with them. It is the same habit as the fan-out row count in session 7: do not trust a number until you can explain it from smaller numbers.

The error message itself suggests format="mixed". That is worth pointing out: following a helpful suggestion is exactly how silent bugs get in.

Fix 4, part two

Parse each format on purpose

How it works

Each line tries one format. Rows it does not fit become NaT, "not a time", and the next line fills only those gaps. The assert proves every row was read by one of the three.

▲

Why dates matter most

A wrong "PHONE" shows up in the first value_counts(). A wrong date looks exactly like a right date. Play 645, the 3rd of August 2024, lands in March, and no total anywhere looks odd.

The counts are 1,793 ISO, 310 with slashes and 120 with dashes, which add up to all 2,223 rows. Checking that the patterns cover every row is part of doing it deliberately.

Fix 5

NA: Namibia, or missing?

pandas decided for you

The text NA, an empty cell, N/A, null and a few others all become missing on a plain read_csv. Usually helpful. A disaster if NA is a country code.

Here it means missing, and we checked

No artist is from Namibia, and the 23 artists with an NA have their real country on their other plays. So it can be filled, from the file itself.

.map(lookup) is the lookup dictionary from the session 4 stretch. The same trick fills the 36 blank genres.

keep_default_na=False takes the decision back: text stays text and an empty cell stays an empty string. That is what load_messy will use at the end of the session, so the decisions are written down rather than guessed.

Before trusting the lookup, check its assumption: one country per artist. The notebook asserts it.

duplicated() marks a row True when an identical row appeared above it. Checking play_id too is the stronger test: a play should exist once, even if a copy was typed differently.

◆

Duplicates move totals a lot and rates hardly at all. The copies added 161.9 minutes, and the skip rate only went from 11.9% to 11.8%. Which one you are reporting decides how much this step matters.

Before the drop_duplicates, the notebook looks: df[df.duplicated(keep=False)] marks every row that has a twin, 80 here, so the pairs show side by side. Look before you delete.

Here both checks say 40, so these are plain copies. In real exports they often are not: the same order exported twice with a different timestamp. That is why checking the id matters more than checking the whole row.

Fix 7, the judgement call

Three ways to handle a gap, three answers

pandas skips NaN when it adds up or averages, which is why leaving and dropping agree.

fillna(0) is a claim

It says 94 plays lasted no time at all. That is a different, false statement, not a neutral placeholder.

dropna() with no arguments is a blunt tool

Any gap in any column drops the row: 159 plays, including 65 with perfectly good minutes that only lacked a device.

Recover, label, or leave

Genre and country were recovered. Device gets a label of its own, "unknown". Minutes stay NaN, which is the number for "nobody knows".

The session 4 stretch had this exact decision with the blank formed_year, and the answer was a label of its own. Ask them which of the three they would choose before showing the right-hand column.

A count that happens to be zero

Nobody planted this

May 2024 had one tablet play, and it is one of the 94 with no minutes. After dropna() the list is empty, so len is 0.

Plain Python stops. pandas carries on with NaN. The loud one is easier to live with: the quiet one ends up as a gap in a chart that looks like you stopped using your tablet.

Session 2 promised "by session 9 you will divide by a count that happens to be zero". This is it, and it fell out of the data rather than being set up. Ask them to recognise the error before you explain it.

Choosing the chart

Bars for categories, a line for time

A line says "this follows from that", which is only true when the x-axis has an order. Genres have none, so they get bars. Months do, so they get a line, and the two summer valleys jump out of 24 numbers.

The quietest months are June to August 2025: 59, 59 and 57 plays. The busiest is October 2024 with 127. Session 4's stretch found the same shape by printing a table; the chart makes it obvious in a second.

▲

Skip rate per device, every number correct in both. The length of a bar is the number, so cutting the bottom off makes 14.0% look many times bigger than 10.6%. Out of 336 speaker plays, the real difference is about eleven skips.

matplotlib starts bar axes at zero by default; the left chart needed set_ylim(10, 14.5) to do the damage. So the lie takes effort, which is worth saying. For lines you can sometimes argue for zooming in. For bars, never.

Out of what? 190 plays are under a minute and skipped. That is 73.6% of the 258 skips, and 84.4% of the 225 short plays: two questions, two answers. Draw the line at two minutes instead and it is 98.4% of skips, but only 65.0% of short plays.

"Short" is under one minute, the line the session 6 stretch drew with CASE WHEN minutes_played < 1. Read the two sentences aloud, "most skips are short" and "most short plays are skips", and ask which the chart supports. Then move the line to two minutes: the first becomes 98.4% and the second drops to 65.0%. A percentage in a title needs its "out of what".

The scatter in notebook 02 is plays against minutes per artist: Sara Lindqvist has the most plays (242), Glass Tram the most minutes (1,175.1), because a Glass Tram play averages 5.1 minutes against 3.2.
