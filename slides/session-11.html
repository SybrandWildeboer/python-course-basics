Part one · 30 minutes

Choosing the question

You propose. I will be annoying about scope, and you will thank me in a fortnight.

The target

What a good project looks like at this level

◆

"Using dataset X, answer question Y, producing a chart and a short written conclusion, generated by a script that runs start to finish."

Three requirements
  • You already have the data, or can download it in ten minutes
  • It uses a database, pandas, and at least one chart
  • It is answerable in the hours you actually have, not the hours you wish you had
What makes a question good
  • It has an answer, and you do not already know it
  • You would be interested in either outcome
  • You can say what "done" means in one sentence

"Would you be interested in either outcome" is a good filter. A question where only one answer is interesting is usually an argument rather than an analysis.

Be honest

First ideas are three projects wearing a coat

"A dashboard of my spending with predictions"

That is a data pipeline, a web app, and a forecasting model. Any one of them is a fortnight.

Scoped: "Which three categories grew most between 2024 and 2025, and by how much?"

One question, one chart, one conclusion. Finishable, and it is the foundation of the ambitious version anyway.

"Analyse the football data and find interesting patterns"

No question, so there is no "done". You would still be poking at it in March.

Scoped: "Do teams that play more home games early in the season finish higher?"

Specific, has an answer, and you will learn more from it precisely because it can be wrong.

◆

The test: can you say what "done" means in one sentence? If not, it is not a project yet, it is an area of interest.

Push hard here, and be warm about it. Enthusiasm is the raw material; your job is to aim it at something that can be finished. Scope creep disguised as ambition is the most common way these projects die.

Agree it out loud

Minimum, target, stretch

Minimum

What counts as done. If you only do this, the project is finished and you present it without apology.

Usually: load the data, answer the question, one chart, a paragraph.

Target

What we both expect. The minimum, plus the things that make it genuinely good.

Usually: proper cleaning, two or three charts, a README somebody else could follow.

Stretch

Only if everything goes well. Written down so it does not accidentally become the target.

A second question, a comparison, something you are curious about.

◆

Write all three into your README today. The point of naming the minimum is that a bad fortnight still ends in a finished project, and the point of naming the stretch is that it stays optional even when it is the most interesting bit.

Say the minimum out loud and get agreement on it. That agreement is what protects them if work or life eats a week, and it is worth more than any amount of encouragement.

Part two · 30 minutes

Set it up, now

Your hands, your keyboard. We are not leaving this section without a run.

Do it now

Eight steps to a running project

  1. Create the repo on GitHub, empty, then clone it.
  2. Copy the structure from project/ in the course repo: data/, notebooks/, src/, output/, README, .gitignore.
  3. python3 -m venv .venv, activate it, install pandas and matplotlib, pip freeze > requirements.txt.
  4. Write the question at the top of the README, along with minimum, target and stretch.
  5. Put the data in data/. Note where it came from in data/README.md.
  6. Write a script that loads the data and prints the row count. Nothing else.
  7. Run it.
  8. Commit and push.
◆

Step 6 sounds too small to bother with. It is the most valuable step in the session: it proves the whole chain works, from the repo to the venv to the data to the script. Everything after it is a change to something that already runs.

Do not let them skip ahead to the analysis. The row count is the deliverable for this block, and it wants to be committed and pushed before you move on.

If their data is a CSV rather than a database, that is fine. The session 6 and 7 material still applies through pd.read_sql if they want a database, and loading a CSV into SQLite is ten lines if it helps them.

The deliverable for this block

The least impressive script you will ever write

What this proves

  • the repo exists and you can push to it
  • the venv works and pandas is in it
  • the data is where you think it is
  • the path works from any folder
  • the file runs

That is five things that could each have eaten an evening, eliminated before you leave the room. And the shape is already right, so every later step is filling in a function.

Printing dtypes as well is worth the extra line. It is the first-five-minutes ritual from session 8, and it often reveals immediately that a date or a number came in as text.

Have them run it and commit it before the break. A pushed commit on day one changes how the fortnight feels.

Part three · 45 minutes

Plan a little, then start

Fifteen minutes of planning, thirty minutes of building. In that order, and not the other way round.

Fifteen minutes, no more

Four or five milestones, with rough dates

By whenMilestone
TodayRepo, venv, data in place, row count printing
Day 3load() and clean() done, types correct, row count checked before and after
Day 6The analysis function that answers the question, returning a table
Day 9One chart, saved, properly labelled
Day 12README written, conclusion written, delete-and-rerun test passes
Day 14Tidy up, reread your own code, stretch goal if there is time

Why the dates matter

Not for accountability. So that when day 9 arrives and you have no chart, you notice in time to cut the stretch goal instead of running out of fortnight.

Notice the shape

It is the pipeline from last session, one function at a time. You are not designing anything new; you are filling in a structure you already have.

Write the conclusion on day 12, not day 14. It always takes longer than people expect, and it is the part that turns a script into a project.

Keep this to fifteen minutes even if it feels unfinished. Time spent planning past that point is time not spent hitting the first real obstacle while you are still in the room.

Your turn · 30 minutes

Start milestone one, with me here

◆ Live work

Get as far into load() and clean() as you can

What to do

  • Load the real data properly, with the right types
  • info(), describe(), value_counts() on the categories
  • Write down what is wrong with it, in the clean() docstring
  • Fix the first problem

Why now and not at home

Because this is where the surprises are. Your data will have something odd about it, and hitting that while somebody is sitting with you is worth more than any amount of advice about what might go wrong.

◆

Explore in a notebook in notebooks/, then move what settles into src/. That is the session 10 pairing, on your own project this time.

Stay quiet and let them work. The goal is that they hit their first real obstacle here. If the data turns out to be unusable, that is the most valuable possible outcome of this session and there is still time to change dataset.

  • We walk through the code together as a real review
  • We pick two or three things and improve them there and then
  • Then a map of where to go next

I am telling you now because people write noticeably tidier code when they know somebody will read it back to them. That is not a trick, it is just true.

Confirm the date and time explicitly and watch them add it to the calendar. Two-week gaps are where sessions get lost.

Say the "tidier code" line honestly. It lands better as an observation than as a threat.

Fair warning

Three things that will happen in the next fortnight

Your data will surprise you

A column will mean something other than what you assumed, or the gaps will not be where you expected. This is normal, and it is the actual work.

What to do: write it down in the README and carry on. Do not change the question unless you have to.

You will want to add something

Halfway through, a much more interesting question will occur to you. It always does.

What to do: write it in the stretch list and finish the original. An interesting unfinished project is worth less than a modest finished one.

You will lose an evening to something stupid

A path, a type, an environment. Everybody does, forever. It is not a sign you are bad at this.

What to do: after twenty minutes stuck, send me the error. That is what the between-sessions channel is for.

◆

And one that will not happen: you will not run out of things you know how to do. Everything your project needs is something you have already done once, in a session, with the answers in the repo.

Naming these three in advance is worth a lot. They each feel like personal failure in the moment, and they are all completely ordinary.
